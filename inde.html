Pax Silica
Is the Philippines Equipped to Handle the Fragile Future of Global Silicon Dynamics?

    The story of Project Pax Silica in the Philippines,formally designated as a 1,620-hectare Economic Security Zone inside New Clark City is a narrative driven by global supply chain crises, geopolitical maneuvering over tech dominance, and local economic ambition.

      Pax Silica members include the United States, Japan, Australia, India, Israel, South Korea, the United Kingdom, and several European nations, with the Netherlands and the EU among the more recent entrants. The Philippines signed on in April 2026 as roughly the 13th member, becoming one of only two Southeast Asian countries in the coalition alongside Singapore.

      The case against it

      Since the Philippines' entry into the coalition, opposition has gathered momentum among a broad constituency—including agrarian organizations, local residents, environmental experts, and government representatives—whose critiques align around several key themes.

      Mass displacement

      Projections from civil society organizations indicate that the development of New Clark City could displace approximately 20,000 residents and 15,000 farmers. Analysts emphasize that informal land tenure among indigenous and generational farming communities is being leveraged to delegitimize customary land rights rather than safeguard local livelihoods.

      Water scarcity

      Concerns persist over the capacity of the Sacobia watershed, which has exhibited signs of hydrological strain since 2020. Environmental advocates contend that the high water requirements of an expanded industrial and semiconductor ecosystem could aggravate water stress in adjacent communities, contrasting with government projections of supply adequacy and claims of prioritized local allocation.

      Mining expansion and pollution

      Given the project’s reliance on critical minerals, critics worry it will drive further large-scale mining in vulnerable provinces like Zambales, Palawan, and Nueva Vizcaya—regions already grappling with deforestation, polluted waterways, and toxic mine tailings. Raising additional alarms, the peasant group Kilusang Magbubukid ng Pilipinas (KMP) cited the historical soil and groundwater pollution left behind by former U.S. military bases in Subic and Clark as a cautionary lesson for these new developments.

      Food security

      A major concern raised by critics is that turning fertile agricultural and coastal zones into industrial and mining hubs threatens national food security, given that these areas currently produce vital crops like rice and coconut.

      Economic dependency

      Detractors characterized the agreement as a significant concession, contending that it relegates the domestic economy to providing inexpensive raw inputs and lower-value processing—specifically assembly, testing, and packaging—while leaving high-value intellectual property, executive control, and financial returns in foreign hands.

      Sovereignty and legal jurisdiction

      Tensions escalated in May 2026 following disclosures by BCDA head Joshua Bingcang regarding U.S. proposals for extraterritorial legal application and diplomatic immunity within the zone, which Philippine authorities promptly declined. While the U.S. State Department later downplayed the comments as a misunderstanding focused on investor predictability, the revelation intensified domestic apprehensions. Citing these concerns, the Makabayan bloc introduced a House resolution highlighting the risks of heightened foreign leverage over sovereign land.
